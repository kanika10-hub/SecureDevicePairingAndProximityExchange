==============================================================
  THE MATHS BEHIND EVERY ALGORITHM WE USED
  From absolute basics — explained for a professor
==============================================================

Before diving into each algorithm, we need three basic ideas that
every single algorithm in this project builds on.


==============================================================
  FOUNDATION 1: MODULAR ARITHMETIC  ("clock maths")
==============================================================

Normal arithmetic: 5 + 9 = 14
Modular arithmetic: 5 + 9 = 2  (mod 12)

Think of a clock. After 12, you wrap around. 13 o'clock = 1 o'clock.
The "mod" operation just means: divide by the number, keep the remainder.

  17 mod 5 = 2   (because 17 = 3×5 + 2)
  25 mod 7 = 4   (because 25 = 3×7 + 4)
  12 mod 12 = 0  (clock: 12 hours later is back to 0)

WHY IT MATTERS FOR CRYPTO:
Modular arithmetic creates "one-way" operations — easy to compute forward,
practically impossible to reverse.

Example:
  3^x mod 17 = 12
  What is x?   → Very hard to find.

  This is called the "Discrete Logarithm Problem."
  It is the foundation of classical cryptography.


==============================================================
  FOUNDATION 2: THE CONCEPT OF A "TRAPDOOR FUNCTION"
==============================================================

A trapdoor function is:
  - Easy to compute in one direction
  - Practically impossible to reverse WITHOUT a secret piece of information
    (the "trapdoor")

Example:
  Multiply two large prime numbers:
    97 × 83 = 8051       ← Easy (milliseconds)
    8051 = ? × ?         ← Which primes? Hard (could take years for huge numbers)

  The trapdoor: knowing one of the prime factors.

Every algorithm in this project is built on a different trapdoor function.


==============================================================
  FOUNDATION 3: WHAT "SECURE" MEANS IN CRYPTO
==============================================================

We say an algorithm is "computationally secure" if:
  Breaking it would require more computing power or time than exists
  in the universe before the heat death of the universe.

This does NOT mean "impossible." It means "infeasible."
  For a 128-bit key: trying every possible key at 1 trillion tries/second
  would take 10^19 years. (Universe is ~13.8 × 10^9 years old.)

This is why key size matters: bigger keys = more time to brute-force.

  ALGORITHM 1: X25519
  (Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman)
  Used for: classical shared secret in pairing + proximity

==============================================================
==============================================================


PART A: WHAT IS A DIFFIE-HELLMAN KEY EXCHANGE?
------------------------------------------------
Invented by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976.
The problem they solved: how do two people agree on a shared secret
over a channel that everyone can listen to?

THE PAINT ANALOGY (the textbook way to explain it):
  1. Alice and Bob agree publicly on a starting colour: YELLOW
  2. Alice secretly picks RED.   Bob secretly picks BLUE.
  3. Alice mixes yellow + red → ORANGE.  She sends orange to Bob.
     Bob mixes yellow + blue → GREEN.    He sends green to Alice.
  4. Alice takes green + her secret red → BROWN.
     Bob takes orange + his secret blue → BROWN.
  5. Both arrive at BROWN, but an eavesdropper only ever saw:
     yellow, orange, green. They cannot figure out the final colour.

The maths equivalent:
  Let p be a large prime number (public).
  Let g be a generator (public, small number like 2 or 5).

  Alice picks private a.  Bob picks private b.
  Alice sends:  A = g^a mod p   (her "public key")
  Bob sends:    B = g^b mod p   (his "public key")

  Alice computes: B^a mod p = (g^b)^a mod p = g^(ab) mod p
  Bob computes:   A^b mod p = (g^a)^b mod p = g^(ab) mod p

  Both get g^(ab) mod p — the shared secret.
  Eve (eavesdropper) sees g, p, g^a, g^b.
  To get g^(ab) she must solve the Discrete Logarithm Problem:
  "Given g^a mod p, find a." This is computationally infeasible for large p.

PROBLEM WITH THIS VERSION:
  Large prime numbers require very large key sizes (2048+ bits) to be secure.
  For devices with limited memory or battery, this is expensive.
  Solution: use a different mathematical structure — elliptic curves.


PART B: WHAT IS AN ELLIPTIC CURVE?
-------------------------------------
An elliptic curve is defined by the equation:
  y² = x³ + ax + b

This is NOT an ellipse. The name is historical (came from calculating
arc lengths of ellipses).

When plotted, these curves have a special shape — symmetric about the x-axis,
with a smooth continuous body.

The KEY property is: you can define ADDITION on points of the curve.

GEOMETRIC POINT ADDITION:
  Given two points P and Q on the curve:
  Draw a line through P and Q.
  It intersects the curve at a third point.
  Reflect that point over the x-axis.
  That reflected point is P + Q.

  Special case (doubling): if P = Q, draw the tangent line at P instead.

ALGEBRAIC FORMULA:
  If P = (x₁, y₁) and Q = (x₂, y₂) and P ≠ Q:
    slope m = (y₂ - y₁) / (x₂ - x₁)
    x₃ = m² - x₁ - x₂
    y₃ = m(x₁ - x₃) - y₁
    P + Q = (x₃, y₃)

All of this is done modulo a large prime p (wrapping around, clock-style).

SCALAR MULTIPLICATION:
  k × P means "add P to itself k times"
  k × P = P + P + P + ... + P  (k times)

  This can be computed efficiently using "double-and-add":
  Like computing 2^10 = ((((2²)²)²)... — squaring repeatedly.
  For k × P: repeatedly double P and conditionally add.
  This makes computing k × P fast (logarithmic time in k).

THE TRAPDOOR:
  Given P and k × P, find k.
  This is the "Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem" (ECDLP).
  No efficient classical algorithm exists for this on the right curve.
  Result: elliptic curves give the same security as RSA with MUCH smaller
  keys. 256-bit ECC ≈ 3072-bit RSA in security level.


PART C: CURVE25519 SPECIFICALLY
---------------------------------
Designed by Daniel Bernstein (2006).
Equation: y² = x³ + 486662x² + x  (over the prime p = 2²⁵⁵ - 19)

Why 2²⁵⁵ - 19?
  - It's a prime number just below a power of 2
  - Numbers can be represented in 255 bits = efficient on 64-bit computers
  - Special form makes modular reduction (the mod operation) very fast

The name "25519" comes from "255" (bit length) and "19" (the constant
subtracted from the prime).

Key sizes:
  Private key: 32 bytes (256 bits) — a random 256-bit number
  Public key:  32 bytes (256 bits) — the x-coordinate of k × G
               (G is the "generator point", a fixed public starting point)

X25519 SPECIFICALLY (the key exchange protocol):
  Alice's private key: a (random 32 bytes)
  Alice's public key:  A = a × G

  Bob's private key:   b (random 32 bytes)
  Bob's public key:    B = b × G

  Shared secret:       a × B = a × (b × G) = ab × G
                       b × A = b × (a × G) = ab × G
  Both sides get the same point! Only the x-coordinate is used as secret.

WHY X25519 SPECIFICALLY (not other curves)?
  - Resistant to timing attacks by design (constant-time computation)
  - No patent issues
  - Simpler implementation, less room for bugs
  - Used by TLS 1.3, Signal, WhatsApp, SSH

  ALGORITHM 2: ML-KEM-768
  (Module Lattice Key Encapsulation Mechanism)
  Used for: post-quantum shared secret — quantum-proof layer
  Standard: NIST FIPS 203, August 2024

==============================================================
==============================================================


PART A: WHY DO WE NEED THIS? (THE QUANTUM THREAT)
----------------------------------------------------
Classical computers solve discrete log by trying factors systematically.
A quantum computer, using Shor's Algorithm (1994), can solve discrete log
and factoring EXPONENTIALLY faster.

IMPACT:
  Classical computer breaking 256-bit ECC: ~2^128 operations (safe forever)
  Quantum computer breaking 256-bit ECC: ~2^85 operations (feasible 2035-2040)

This means X25519, RSA, and all classical key exchange become insecure
once sufficiently powerful quantum computers exist.

ML-KEM is based on a completely different mathematical problem that
quantum computers (as far as we know) CANNOT solve efficiently.


PART B: WHAT IS A LATTICE?
-----------------------------
A lattice is a grid of points that extends infinitely in all directions.

In 2D, the simplest lattice is the integer grid:
  { (x, y) : x, y are integers }
  ...(-2,-2), (-1,-2), (0,-2), (1,-2), (2,-2)...
  ...(-2,-1), (-1,-1), (0,-1), (1,-1), (2,-1)...
  ...(-2, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 0), (1, 0), (2, 0)...
  etc.

In cryptography, we use high-dimensional lattices (256, 512, 1024 dimensions).
Think of an integer grid but in 1024-dimensional space.

A lattice can also be defined by a "basis" — a set of vectors from which
all lattice points can be reached by integer combinations.


PART C: THE HARD PROBLEM — LWE (Learning With Errors)
-------------------------------------------------------
LWE was introduced by Oded Regev in 2005.

THE SETUP:
  Let n = some dimension (say, 256).
  Let q = a small prime (say, 3329 for ML-KEM).
  Let s = a secret vector in Z_q^n  (a list of n numbers, each mod q).

THE SAMPLES:
  Compute many "noisy" equations:
    b₁ = a₁ · s + e₁  (mod q)
    b₂ = a₂ · s + e₂  (mod q)
    b₃ = a₃ · s + e₃  (mod q)
    ...

  Where:
    aᵢ = a random vector (public)
    bᵢ = the result (public)
    eᵢ = a tiny random "error" term (secret, small number like ±1 or ±2)

  If there were NO error:
    b = a · s  → you could solve for s using linear algebra (Gaussian elimination)

  WITH the tiny errors:
    The equations are "almost" linear but not quite.
    No efficient algorithm (classical or quantum) is known to find s.
    This is the LWE problem.

THE KEY INSIGHT:
  Small errors destroy the algebraic structure that makes linear systems
  solvable. The problem becomes as hard as finding the shortest vector in
  a high-dimensional lattice — one of the oldest unsolved problems in
  computational geometry.

QUANTUM RESISTANCE:
  Best quantum algorithms (BKZ, sieve algorithms) for LWE:
  Still exponential in the dimension n. A quantum computer doesn't help
  significantly, unlike with discrete logarithm.


PART D: MODULE-LWE (THE STRUCTURED VERSION USED IN ML-KEM)
------------------------------------------------------------
Plain LWE requires very large keys. Module-LWE adds algebraic structure
using polynomial rings to make keys smaller while keeping hardness.

POLYNOMIAL RING:
  Instead of working with single numbers, we work with polynomials modulo:
    Rq = Z_q[x] / (x^n + 1)

  This means: polynomials of degree up to n-1, with coefficients mod q.
  In ML-KEM-768: n = 256, q = 3329.

  A polynomial looks like:
    a(x) = a₀ + a₁x + a₂x² + ... + a₂₅₅x²⁵⁵
    where each aᵢ is an integer from 0 to 3328.

  Multiplication in this ring: multiply polynomials normally,
  then reduce mod (x^256 + 1), and reduce all coefficients mod 3329.

MODULE-LWE:
  Work with VECTORS of such polynomials.
  The "768" in ML-KEM-768 means: we use vectors of length 3
  (3 × 256 = 768 polynomial coefficients total per vector).
  Security level 3 out of 5 in NIST's classification.


PART E: HOW ML-KEM ACTUALLY WORKS (KEY ENCAPSULATION)
-------------------------------------------------------
This is a Key Encapsulation Mechanism (KEM). It does NOT work like DH.
Instead:

KEY GENERATION (Device A, done once during pairing):
  1. Generate random secret vector s (the private key) — small coefficients
  2. Generate random matrix A (public)
  3. Add small error vector e
  4. Compute: pk = A·s + e    (over the polynomial ring, mod q)
  Private key = s
  Public key  = (A, pk)

ENCAPSULATION (Device B does this when Device A's public key is known):
  1. Generate random message m (just 32 random bytes initially)
  2. Use m to derive randomness for the operation (deterministic from m)
  3. Generate random vector r, error vectors e1, e2
  4. Compute:
       u = A·r + e1     (like a "noisy" version of A·r)
       v = pk·r + e2 + encode(m)  (embed the message into the noise)
  5. Output: ciphertext c = (u, v) and the encapsulated key K = Hash(m)

DECAPSULATION (Device A does this using its private key s):
  1. Compute: w = v - s·u
     = (pk·r + e2 + encode(m)) - s·(A·r + e1)
     = (A·s + e)·r + e2 + encode(m) - s·A·r - s·e1
     = A·s·r - s·A·r + e·r + e2 - s·e1 + encode(m)
     = 0 + (small noise) + encode(m)
  2. The noise terms are small (because e, e1, e2 were small).
  3. decode(w) recovers m with high probability.
  4. Recompute K = Hash(m).

WHY DECAPSULATION WORKS:
  The errors are small. When Device A computes v - s·u, the error terms
  are small enough to not change the overall decoded value. The message m
  sits on top of the noise, big enough to survive.

SECURITY:
  An eavesdropper sees ciphertext c = (u, v) but not s.
  To recover m, they must solve Module-LWE: find s from (A, A·s + e).
  No known efficient classical or quantum algorithm for this.

KEY SIZES IN ML-KEM-768:
  Public key:  1184 bytes   (3 polynomial vectors)
  Private key: 2400 bytes   (more material stored for decapsulation)
  Ciphertext:  1088 bytes   (u + v compressed)
  Shared key:   32 bytes

  ALGORITHM 3: HKDF-SHA256
  (HMAC-based Key Derivation Function)
  Used for: combining X25519 and ML-KEM secrets into one session key

==============================================================
==============================================================


PART A: WHAT IS A HASH FUNCTION?
-----------------------------------
A hash function takes any input (any size) and outputs a fixed-size string.

Properties of a CRYPTOGRAPHIC hash:
  1. Deterministic:  same input → always same output
  2. Fast to compute
  3. Pre-image resistant:  given output H, cannot find input m such that H(m) = H
  4. Collision resistant:  cannot find two different inputs with the same output
  5. Avalanche effect:     change one bit of input → ~50% of output bits change

Example with SHA-256 (256-bit output):
  SHA-256("hello")     = 2cf24dba5fb0a30e26e83b2ac5b9e29e1b161e5c1fa7425e73043362938b9824
  SHA-256("helo")      = dffd6021bb2bd5b0af676290809ec3a53191dd81c7f70a4b28688a362182986d
                         ^completely different despite one letter change

WHAT SHA-256 DOES INTERNALLY:
  (The "Merkle-Damgård" compression function structure)
  1. Pad input to a multiple of 512 bits
  2. Split into 512-bit blocks
  3. Initialise 8 state words (H₀...H₇) with fixed constants
     (first digits of the fractional parts of square roots of first 8 primes)
  4. For each 512-bit block, run 64 rounds of mixing:
     Each round uses bitwise AND, OR, XOR, NOT, and bit-rotations
     and adds a "round constant" (digits of cube roots of first 64 primes)
  5. Add the result to the previous state words (Davies-Meyer step)
  6. Final 256-bit state = the hash

The mixing is designed so the output is indistinguishable from random.


PART B: WHAT IS HMAC?
-----------------------
HMAC = Hash-based Message Authentication Code.
Used to verify BOTH the content AND the origin of a message.

Construction:
  HMAC(key, message) = Hash(key_outer ∥ Hash(key_inner ∥ message))

  Where:
    key_inner = key XOR ipad   (ipad = 0x36 repeated)
    key_outer = key XOR opad   (opad = 0x5C repeated)
    ∥ means concatenation

WHY TWO HASH CALLS?
  A single Hash(key ∥ message) is vulnerable to "length extension attacks"
  (due to Merkle-Damgård's structure). The double-hash prevents this.

SECURITY:
  Without the key, you cannot compute the HMAC. Even knowing the message,
  you cannot fake the HMAC for any modified version.
  This gives us "data integrity + authentication" in one operation.


PART C: HKDF
--------------
HKDF (RFC 5869) turns "source key material" (which might not be uniform)
into a uniformly random key of any desired length.

TWO PHASES:

  PHASE 1 — Extract:
    Takes the raw key material (possibly non-uniform) and "extracts" a
    pseudorandom key (PRK) using HMAC.
    PRK = HMAC(salt, input_key_material)
    If no salt is given, use a block of zeros.

  PHASE 2 — Expand:
    Uses the PRK to produce output key material of any desired length.
    OKM = T(1) ∥ T(2) ∥ T(3) ∥ ...
    Where:
      T(1) = HMAC(PRK, info ∥ 0x01)
      T(2) = HMAC(PRK, T(1) ∥ info ∥ 0x02)
      T(3) = HMAC(PRK, T(2) ∥ info ∥ 0x03)
      ...
    "info" is a label string (we use "hybrid-session-key" or
    "proximity-ephemeral-session") that domain-separates different uses.

HOW WE USE IT:
  X25519 produces a 32-byte shared secret (not necessarily uniform).
  ML-KEM produces a 32-byte shared secret.
  We concatenate them: IKM = X25519_secret ∥ ML-KEM_secret  (64 bytes)
  Then: session_key = HKDF-SHA256(IKM, info="hybrid-session-key")
  Output: 16 bytes (128 bits) — exactly the size ASCON-128a needs.

WHY CONCATENATE THEN HKDF?
  This is the standard hybrid KEM construction used by Signal (PQXDH) and
  TLS 1.3 hybrid drafts. Security guarantee:
  "As long as AT LEAST ONE of the component secrets is uncompromised,
  the derived session key is secure."
  An attacker must break BOTH X25519 AND ML-KEM simultaneously.

  ALGORITHM 4: ASCON-128a
  (Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data)
  Used for: encrypting every single message in the system
  Standard: NIST SP 800-232, 2023

==============================================================
==============================================================


PART A: WHAT IS AEAD?
-----------------------
AEAD = Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data.
This is what we actually use to lock messages. It provides:

  CONFIDENTIALITY: nobody can read the message without the key.
  INTEGRITY:       any modification of the ciphertext is detected.
  AUTHENTICATION:  proves the message came from someone who has the key.

Associated Data (AD):
  Data that you want to AUTHENTICATE but NOT encrypt.
  For example: a header, a nonce, a session ID.
  AD is checked against the tag but not hidden.


PART B: THE SPONGE CONSTRUCTION (foundation of ASCON)
-------------------------------------------------------
Most hash functions (SHA-256) use Merkle-Damgård.
ASCON uses a "Sponge" construction.

SPONGE ANALOGY:
  Imagine a sponge with a "rate" (the part you can touch) and a
  "capacity" (the hidden inner core).

  ABSORBING (input):
    XOR the input data into the "rate" part.
    Apply the permutation function p (a mixing function).
    Repeat for each block of input.

  SQUEEZING (output):
    Read out the "rate" part as output.
    Apply permutation again.
    Repeat until you have enough output bits.

STATE:
  ASCON has a 320-bit internal state (5 words of 64 bits each).
  Rate   = 128 bits  (the "rate" — data enters and exits here)
  Capacity = 192 bits  (the hidden core — never directly exposed)

  The capacity is the security parameter. An attacker who sees the
  rate output cannot reconstruct the capacity without inverting the
  permutation — which is computationally infeasible.


PART C: THE ASCON PERMUTATION (pA, pB)
----------------------------------------
ASCON uses a 12-round (pA) or 8-round (pB) permutation.
Each round has THREE STEPS:

STEP 1 — Constant Addition:
  XOR a round constant into one word of the 320-bit state.
  (Different constant each round — prevents slide attacks.)

STEP 2 — Substitution Layer (S-box):
  Apply a 5-bit S-box to every column of the state simultaneously.
  The state is 5 words of 64 bits = 64 columns of 5 bits each.
  Each 5-bit input → a different 5-bit output (lookup table, nonlinear).
  This provides CONFUSION (obscures relationship between key and output).

  The ASCON S-box (in binary):
    Input:  00000→00000, 00001→00100, 00010→01011, ...
    (a fixed nonlinear 5-bit lookup table chosen for good algebraic properties)

STEP 3 — Linear Diffusion Layer:
  Each of the 5 words is XOR-ed with rotated copies of itself.
  For word x₀:  x₀ = x₀ XOR (x₀ >>> 19) XOR (x₀ >>> 28)
  For word x₁:  x₁ = x₁ XOR (x₁ >>> 61) XOR (x₁ >>> 39)
  (each word has different rotation constants)
  This provides DIFFUSION (each bit of input affects all bits of output).

WHY THIS IS SECURE:
  After enough rounds, any change in input propagates to all bits of the
  state. An attacker cannot find patterns because the S-box destroys linear
  structure, and diffusion spreads changes everywhere.


PART D: ASCON-128a FULL ENCRYPTION FLOW
-----------------------------------------
Parameters:
  Key size:   128 bits  (16 bytes)
  Nonce size: 128 bits  (16 bytes) — must be UNIQUE per encryption
  Rate:       128 bits  (16 bytes per block)
  Tag size:   128 bits  (16 bytes, appended to ciphertext)

STEP 1 — INITIALIZATION:
  Load initial state: IV ∥ Key ∥ Nonce  (320 bits total)
  IV = a fixed constant identifying the variant (Ascon-128a).
  Run permutation pA (12 rounds).
  XOR the Key into the bottom 128 bits of the state.
  State is now "keyed and initialized."

STEP 2 — PROCESS ASSOCIATED DATA (if any):
  Absorb the associated data (AD) in 128-bit blocks:
    XOR each block into the rate, run pB (8 rounds).
  After all AD is absorbed:
    XOR a 1-bit into the state (domain separator: "AD is done").

STEP 3 — ENCRYPTION:
  For each 128-bit block of plaintext:
    XOR the plaintext block into the rate.
    The rate now IS the ciphertext block. Output it.
    Run pB (8 rounds).
  (Partial last block: only XOR the available bits, pad with zeros,
   output only the same number of bits as plaintext length.)

STEP 4 — FINALIZATION:
  XOR the Key back into the state.
  Run pA (12 rounds).
  XOR the Key into the final state.
  Output the last 128 bits of the state as the authentication TAG.

DECRYPTION:
  Reverse step 3: XOR ciphertext into rate → get plaintext.
  Recompute the tag from scratch.
  Compare computed tag against received tag using CONSTANT-TIME comparison.
  If tags don't match → reject everything. Output nothing.

THE CONSTANT-TIME COMPARISON:
  You CANNOT compare tags with a normal "==" check, because:
  Some languages/CPUs short-circuit and return false as soon as one byte
  differs. An attacker can time how long the comparison takes to deduce
  HOW MANY bytes matched, which leaks information about the real tag.
  Constant-time comparison always compares all 16 bytes in the same time.


PART E: WHY ASCON OVER AES?
------------------------------
AES (Advanced Encryption Standard):
  Block cipher, 128-bit blocks, 128/192/256-bit keys.
  Uses 10/12/14 rounds of SubBytes + ShiftRows + MixColumns + AddRoundKey.
  Extremely fast WITH hardware support (AES-NI instructions on Intel/AMD CPUs).
  Problem: on a microcontroller with no AES-NI, AES is slow and code is large.

ASCON:
  Sponge construction, 320-bit state, designed for SOFTWARE.
  No lookup tables (S-box is computed with 5 AND/XOR/NOT operations).
  Tiny code footprint (~2 KB compiled).
  Designed for IoT chips with 4 KB RAM and no hardware acceleration.
  Won NIST Lightweight Crypto competition (2019-2023) against 56 candidates.
  The S-box has 0 fixed points and is 5-bit (trivially protects against
  timing side-channels because there are no memory lookups at all).

For this project's use case (devices that might be Bluetooth sensors or
wearables), ASCON is the architecturally correct choice.

==============================================================

  ALGORITHM 5: HMAC-SHA256 (for the MAC in proximity protocol)
  Used for: authenticating ephemeral keys during reconnect

==============================================================
==============================================================

We already explained HMAC above. Here's how it's specifically used:

During proximity reconnect, Device A needs to PROVE it's the same device
that completed the original QR pairing, WITHOUT re-doing the full QR scan.

HOW IT WORKS:
  After pairing, both devices share a long-term secret:
    DH_result = X25519(my_long_term_priv, peer_long_term_pub)
  This gives the same 32-byte value on both sides (DH commutativity).
  We then HKDF this into a "long-term MAC key":
    lt_key = HKDF(DH_result, info="proximity-long-term-mac-key")

  For every reconnect, Device A stamps its fresh ephemeral keys:
    MAC = HMAC-SHA256(lt_key, device_id ∥ eph_x25519_pub ∥ eph_mlkem_pub)

  Device B verifies:
    Compute expected_MAC from the same lt_key.
    Compare with received MAC using constant-time comparison.
    Match → authenticated. No match → rejected.

WHY THIS WORKS:
  Only a device that holds the ORIGINAL long-term private key can compute
  the same DH_result and therefore the same lt_key. An attacker who
  intercepted the WiFi during a reconnect sees MAC values but cannot
  reproduce them without the private key. Even if they replay an old MAC,
  it binds to specific ephemeral public keys — a replayed MAC with different
  keys will not verify.


==============================================================
==============================================================

  ALGORITHM 6: SCRYPT
  (Password-based Key Derivation Function)
  Used for: encrypting identity.json with a passphrase

==============================================================
==============================================================


THE PROBLEM WITH PLAIN PASSWORD HASHING:
  SHA-256("mypassword") = fast to compute (< 1ms).
  An attacker with a GPU can try 10 billion passwords per second.
  Against a dictionary of 1 billion passwords × 1000 variations = 1 second.

THE SOLUTION: DELIBERATE SLOWNESS AND MEMORY HARDNESS:
  Make the computation:
    (a) Deliberately slow (configurable, we target ~100ms)
    (b) Memory-hard: require a lot of RAM, so GPUs (which have little RAM
        per core) can't run millions of instances in parallel

SCRYPT (Colin Percival, 2009):
  Three parameters:
    N = CPU/memory cost (we use 16384 = 2^14)
    r = block size factor (we use 8)
    p = parallelisation factor (we use 1)
    Memory used: 128 × N × r bytes = 128 × 16384 × 8 = 16 MB

HOW SCRYPT WORKS INTERNALLY:
  1. Run PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 to expand the password into p "blocks"
     of (128 × r) bytes each.

  2. For each block, run the "ROMix" function:
     a. Store N sequential hash states in memory (N entries of 128r bytes).
        This requires N × 128r = 16MB RAM simultaneously.
     b. For N more rounds, pick a pseudo-random earlier state from memory
        (determined by the current state) and XOR it in.
        The random access pattern means you CANNOT compute this without
        storing all N states. A GPU with 1 MB per core cannot run this.

  3. Run PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 again to compress to the desired key size.

RESULT:
  Computing scrypt(password) takes ~100ms on a modern CPU and ~16 MB of RAM.
  An attacker can try at most ~10 guesses per second per CPU core.
  Instead of 10 billion guesses/second → 10 guesses/second.
  A dictionary attack that took 1 second now takes 3 years.

WHAT WE STORE:
  A random 16-byte SALT is generated at encryption time.
  salt + parameters (N, r, p) are stored in identity.json in plaintext.
  Only the key itself (and therefore the private keys) needs the passphrase.
  Different salts ensure the same passphrase gives different keys for
  different identity files — prevents precomputation attacks.


==============================================================
  HOW ALL SIX ALGORITHMS CONNECT IN OUR PROJECT
==============================================================

PAIRING (first-time, QR-based):

  [Device A's long-term keypairs]
         X25519 keypair (classical)
         ML-KEM keypair (quantum-proof)
         ↓
  [QR code (out-of-band channel)]
         Carries: device_id + X25519_pub_A + ML-KEM_pub_A
         ↓ scanned by Device B
  [Device B computes session key]
         X25519: shared = DH(B_priv, A_pub) ← classical secret
         ML-KEM: (ciphertext, pq_secret) = Encaps(A_pub) ← quantum secret
         HKDF:   session_key = HKDF(X25519_secret ∥ pq_secret)
         ASCON:  ciphertext = ASCON(session_key, nonce, B's public keys)
         ↓ sends ciphertext + ML-KEM ciphertext to Device A
  [Device A recovers session key]
         ML-KEM: pq_secret = Decaps(A_priv, ML-KEM_ciphertext)
         X25519: shared = DH(A_priv, B_pub)
         HKDF:   session_key = HKDF(X25519_secret ∥ pq_secret) ← SAME KEY
         ASCON:  B's public keys = ASCON_decrypt(session_key, nonce, ciphertext)
  [Both compute fingerprint = SHA-256(both devices' public keys)]
  [Human comparison out loud → confirms authenticity]

PROXIMITY RECONNECT (every subsequent encounter):

  [Device A knows B's long-term public key from pairing]
  [Compute long-term MAC key]
         X25519: lt_shared = DH(A_priv_longterm, B_pub_longterm)
         HKDF:   lt_key = HKDF(lt_shared, info="long-term-mac-key")
  [Device A generates fresh one-time keys]
         New X25519 ephemeral keypair
         New ML-KEM ephemeral keypair
  [Stamp the fresh keys]
         HMAC:   mac = HMAC-SHA256(lt_key, device_id ∥ eph_x25519 ∥ eph_mlkem)
  [Send over WiFi/BLE: device_id + eph_x25519 + eph_mlkem + mac]
  [Device B verifies stamp]
         Looks up A's public key from trust store.
         Computes same lt_key.
         Verifies HMAC.
  [Fresh session key]
         ML-KEM: (kem_ct, pq_secret) = Encaps(A's eph_mlkem_pub)
         X25519: eph_shared = DH(B_eph_priv, A_eph_pub)
         HKDF:   session_key = HKDF(eph_shared ∥ pq_secret, "proximity-session")
  [Encrypt token]
         ASCON:  token_encrypted = ASCON(session_key, fresh_nonce, token)
  [Keys discarded after session — forward secrecy]

IDENTITY PROTECTION AT REST:

  passphrase
         ↓ Scrypt(salt, N=16384, r=8, p=1)
  16-byte key
         ↓ ASCON-128a encrypt(key, nonce, private_keys, AD=device_id+pub_keys)
  Encrypted blob stored in identity.json

SECURITY LEVELS (NIST classification):
  Our system targets "Security Level 3" — ML-KEM-768's classification.
  This means: breaking it requires ~2^128 quantum operations.
  128-bit post-quantum security is the current recommended minimum for
  systems that should stay secure beyond 2030.


==============================================================
  THE KEY INTUITION IN ONE PAGE
==============================================================

The entire system rests on five mathematical facts:

FACT 1: Elliptic curve discrete log is hard.
  Given G and k×G, you cannot find k.
  This gives us X25519 — two parties independently compute ab×G.

FACT 2: Module-LWE is hard, even for quantum computers.
  Given A and A·s + e (with small error), you cannot find s.
  This gives us ML-KEM — Device B seals a secret only Device A can open.

FACT 3: A cryptographic hash function is a one-way function.
  Given H(x), you cannot find x.
  This gives us HKDF (key mixing), HMAC (authentication stamps),
  and fingerprints (safety numbers).

FACT 4: A pseudorandom permutation with a secret key is indistinguishable
from a truly random permutation.
  ASCON's sponge construction, given the correct key and nonce, produces
  output that looks completely random. No attacker can distinguish real
  ASCON output from random bytes without the key.

FACT 5: Memory-hard functions equalise the playing field.
  Scrypt forces every password guess to use 16 MB of RAM for ~100 ms.
  This removes the GPU/ASIC advantage attackers use to speed up cracking.

PUT TOGETHER:
  - The QR code brings Fact 1 and Fact 2 to the physical world.
    An attacker who was never there cannot break Facts 1 or 2.
  - Fact 3 (HKDF) combines the outputs of Facts 1 and 2 into a key.
  - Fact 4 (ASCON) uses that key to lock the actual messages.
  - Facts 3 and 4 together (HMAC) stamp every reconnect so impersonation
    is impossible without the private key from Fact 1.
  - Fact 5 (Scrypt) protects private keys if someone steals the file.
