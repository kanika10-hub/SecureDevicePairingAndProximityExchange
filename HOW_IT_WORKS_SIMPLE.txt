  SECURE DEVICE PAIRING — HOW IT WORKS (PLAIN ENGLISH)
  Written so anyone can understand it, no tech background needed
===============================================================


WHAT IS THIS PROJECT?
---------------------
Imagine you have two phones. You want them to talk to each other privately
— like passing a secret note — so that nobody in the middle can read it,
change it, or pretend to be you.

That's exactly what this project does. We built a system where:
  1. Two devices meet and shake hands securely for the first time (pairing)
  2. Every time they see each other again, they prove who they are and
     talk in a secret language nobody else can understand (reconnect)
  3. If a device is lost or stolen, you can cut it off immediately (revoke)
  4. There's a live website that shows every single step happening on screen

We built the whole thing from scratch — the security math, the app, the
website, the tests, everything.


WHY DOES THIS MATTER?
---------------------
Right now when your phone connects to another device (say, AirDrop or
Bluetooth), there are three big dangers:

  DANGER 1 — Someone sitting in the middle
    A hacker could pretend to be the other device. You think you're talking
    to your friend's phone, but really you're talking to the hacker.
    We call this a "Man in the Middle" (MITM) attack.
    --> We solved this with a QR code (explained below).

  DANGER 2 — Future quantum computers
    Normal phone encryption uses maths that today's computers can't break.
    But quantum computers (coming in the future) can break it.
    --> We added an extra layer of quantum-proof encryption on top.

  DANGER 3 — Once trusted, always trusted
    Most pairing systems have no off switch. If you lose your device,
    it stays trusted forever.
    --> We built a proper revocation system (like revoking a key card).

--- 2. CORE CRYPTO — THE MATHS ENGINE (core/ folder) ---

This is the engine room. Vikram built four cryptographic tools from scratch:

  a) X25519 (Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman)
     Think of it like this: Alice and Bob both have a paint colour nobody
     else knows. They each mix their secret colour into a shared public
     colour, swap the mixed results, and then add their own secret colour
     again. They end up with the same final colour — but nobody watching
     could figure it out just by looking at the colours that were swapped.
     This is how two devices agree on a secret without ever saying the
     secret out loud.

  b) ML-KEM-768 (Post-Quantum Encryption, NIST standard)
     Normal encryption (like X25519 above) will be cracked by quantum
     computers in the future. ML-KEM-768 is a brand new type of maths
     (approved by the US government's NIST institute in 2024) that even
     quantum computers can't break. We use BOTH X25519 and ML-KEM-768
     together, so you'd have to break TWO different systems simultaneously
     to crack our encryption. That's basically impossible.

  c) HKDF-SHA256 (Key Mixing)
     After X25519 and ML-KEM-768 each produce a secret number, we need to
     turn those two numbers into one single usable key. HKDF is a standard
     recipe for doing that safely — like combining two ingredients into one
     perfect sauce. You can't reverse it to find the original ingredients.

  d) ASCON-128a (The Actual Lock and Key for Messages)
     Once we have a session key, we need to use it to encrypt messages.
     ASCON-128a is the winner of a global encryption competition run by
     NIST (the US standards body) in 2023. It was specifically designed
     for small devices like phones, watches, and sensors — it uses very
     little memory and doesn't need special hardware chips. Every message
     sent between devices is locked with ASCON. It also detects if anyone
     tampered with the message in transit.


--- 3. PAIRING — HOW TWO DEVICES MEET FOR THE FIRST TIME (pairing/ folder) ---

This is the "first handshake." Vikram built the whole process:

  STEP 1: Device A generates its identity
    It creates two keypairs (one classical, one quantum-proof). Think of
    these like a padlock and a quantum padlock, and only Device A has the
    keys to open them.

  STEP 2: Device A shows a QR code
    It encodes its public padlock into a QR code. The QR code is shown on
    the screen physically — you have to point your camera at it.
    WHY QR? Because a hacker sitting on the WiFi network CANNOT see the QR
    code. It's a physical out-of-band channel. They'd have to be physically
    present and looking at your screen.

  STEP 3: Device B scans the QR
    Device B reads Device A's public padlock from the QR.
    It uses that padlock to lock up its own keys and sends them over
    the network. Only Device A can unlock this — nobody else.

  STEP 4: Both devices get a fingerprint
    Both devices independently calculate the same unique "safety number"
    (like a fingerprint of the entire exchange). You read yours out loud,
    they read theirs — if they match, the pairing was secure. This is
    exactly what Signal messenger does when you verify a contact.

  STEP 5: Both save the trust record
    Both devices write down "I trust this peer" in a file. From now on,
    they can recognise each other every time they meet.


--- 4. MITM ATTACK ANALYSIS ---

Vikram also wrote the full security analysis document that maps out:
  - What a "Man in the Middle" attacker can and cannot do
  - Why the QR code stops them at the pairing stage
  - Why even if they record all the network traffic, they can't decrypt it
  - What is still NOT protected (being honest about limits — like if
    someone physically steals your phone and extracts the key files)
  - Why we chose ASCON over AES or DES (ASCON is the modern standard for
    small devices; AES is fine for servers but not designed for constrained
    hardware)

KANIKA'S CONTRIBUTIONS

--- 5. PROXIMITY RECONNECT — FINDING EACH OTHER AGAIN (proximity/ folder) ---

After the first pairing, you don't want to scan a QR code every single
time your devices see each other. Kanika built the automatic reconnect:

  HOW IT WORKS:
    Every time Device A comes near Device B (via WiFi or Bluetooth):

    a) Device A generates FRESH temporary keys just for this one encounter.
       These are thrown away immediately after — so even if someone somehow
       gets them later, they can't decrypt this conversation. This is called
       "forward secrecy."

    b) Device A stamps those temporary keys with a "signature" (technically
       an HMAC — a special fingerprint) made using the long-term secret that
       was established during the original QR pairing. Only a device that
       genuinely completed the QR pairing can produce this stamp.

    c) Device B checks the stamp. If it's valid → "yes, this is my paired
       device, let's talk." If it's wrong or missing → silent rejection.
       The stranger gets no response at all — not even a "no." This is
       deliberate: telling an attacker "access denied" gives them useful
       information. Silence gives them nothing.

    d) Both sides independently arrive at the same session key, and the
       conversation proceeds, fully encrypted with ASCON-128a.

  TRANSPORTS:
    This works over two different wireless technologies:
      - WiFi (mDNS discovery + TCP) — live tested, works end to end
      - Bluetooth BLE — built and unit tested (hardware limitation on
        Windows prevented live BLE testing, so WiFi was used for the demo)


--- 6. TRUST STORE AND STRANGER REJECTION ---

Kanika built the trust management system:

  WHAT IS A TRUST STORE?
    It's like a contacts book, but for cryptographic identities. When you
    pair with a device, its name and public keys go into your trust store.
    Every time a device tries to connect, you first check: "are you in
    my book?" If not, you slam the door.

  STRANGER REJECTION:
    If a device you've never paired with tries to connect:
      - Your device looks them up in the trust store → NOT FOUND
      - It does not send back any response
      - The stranger can't tell if your device even exists on the network
    This is completely automatic. You don't need to do anything.

  REVOCATION (the off switch):
    Before this was built, once you paired a device it was trusted FOREVER.
    The only way to unpair was to delete a file manually.

    Now there's a proper off switch:
      - You can REVOKE a device: "stop trusting this device" (e.g. if you
        lost your phone). This is reversible — you can restore it later.
      - You can set an EXPIRY: "trust this device for 30 days, then stop."
      - A revoked device is rejected with the exact same message as a
        complete stranger. The attacker can't tell it was specifically
        revoked — that would leak information.

    Command line example:
      python -m demo.trust_cli device-a --revoke device-b --reason "lost"
      python -m demo.trust_cli device-a --restore device-b


--- 7. WEBSITE — THE LIVE VISUALIZER (website/ folder) ---

Kanika built the interactive website that makes the whole system visible:

  WHAT IT DOES:
    Opens in your browser. You click "Pair Fresh" and watch, step by step,
    exactly what the two devices are doing — the actual hex values of the
    keys, the encrypted ciphertext, the session key, everything. Nothing
    is hidden or simplified away. Every real cryptographic value is shown.

  HOW IT WORKS TECHNICALLY:
    - A FastAPI Python server runs in the background (port 8000)
    - It performs the real crypto operations (not fake/simulated)
    - It streams each step to the browser in real time via WebSocket
      (a persistent live connection, like a phone call between browser
      and server, so updates appear instantly without page refresh)
    - There's also a React version of the frontend (port 3000) with a
      richer interface

  THREE SCENARIOS YOU CAN WATCH LIVE:
    1. "Pair Fresh" — watch two devices meet for the first time
    2. "Reconnect" — watch them find each other again with fresh keys
    3. "Stranger Attack" — watch an unknown device try to connect and
       get silently rejected, with the fake MAC highlighted in red


--- 8. TESTING ATTACK SCENARIOS ---

Kanika wrote tests for the security properties — not just "does it work"
but "does it correctly FAIL when it should":

  - MITM test: an attacker tries to intercept and relay messages
  - Stranger test: an unknown device tries to connect → rejected
  - Reconnect test: previously paired device reconnects → accepted
  - MAC forgery test: attacker tries to fake the authentication stamp
  - Tampered message test: attacker flips a bit in the ciphertext →
    ASCON detects it and refuses to decrypt

These tests run automatically and take under a second each. 131 tests
total in the project.

     Before this, if someone opened the identity.json file on your laptop,
     they could read your private keys in plain text. Kanika added:
       - A passphrase lock on the file
       - The passphrase is turned into a key using Scrypt
         (a deliberately slow function — makes brute-force guessing
         the password take months instead of seconds)
       - The private keys are encrypted with ASCON-128a
       - If you forget the passphrase, the keys are gone forever
         (that's the point — if you could recover without the passphrase,
         so could an attacker)

THE SIMPLE VERSION (what to say to someone non-technical)
==========================================================

Imagine you want to pass a secret note to your friend across a crowded room.

THE PROBLEM: Anyone could grab the note, read it, or swap it with a fake.

OUR SOLUTION:
  - First, you show your friend a QR code up close (so only THEY see it).
    That QR has a secret padlock inside it.
  - Your friend uses that padlock to seal a note back to you. Only you can
    open it. Now you both have a shared secret.
  - Every time you want to talk after that, you each show a little badge
    stamped with that original shared secret. The badge proves "it's really
    me." You then use fresh, one-time-only codes for each conversation.
  - If someone walks up claiming to be your friend but without the badge,
    they're ignored completely. No answer, no acknowledgement — nothing.
  - The notes are locked with a type of encryption that even future
    super-computers (quantum computers) cannot crack.
  - If your friend loses their phone, you can cancel their badge instantly.

That's it. We built all of that in Python, running on real WiFi, with
a website where you can watch every step happen live in your browser.
